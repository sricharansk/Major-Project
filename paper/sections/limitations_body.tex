\label{sec:limitations}
\textit{Data.} The DOT link set is sparse and highway-heavy; the full-year link enumeration timed out server-side, so
the study uses the 26 Manhattan links active since 2026-09-20, of which seven are dead feeds, three more have under
3\,\% coverage, three lose their feed before or during the test partition (NOT\_EVALUATED) and 16 are modelled; about
half of all raw records are zero-speed no-measurement rows, treated as missing. Monitored segments do not tile the
highways and the published polylines stop short of the named link ends, so the graph needs name-based continuity
rules and remains sparse (16 directed edges, 6 isolated nodes before eligibility). The eligibility threshold was
lowered from the planned 0.6 to 0.5 training coverage after the coverage audit and before any result on the 0.6 set
existed; both are recorded. Operational weather is Open-Meteo's historical-forecast product, a re-forecast proxy
rather than an archived agency forecast. Collisions lag publication by months and are retrospective only.

\textit{Modelling and evaluation.} All deep models were trained under a recorded budget of 4{,}096 windows per epoch
with early stopping and one chronological split. The three deep models were compared over only three seeds
(Table~\ref{tab:multiseed}); the day-block bootstrap describes test-day variation of the seed-averaged errors and does
not estimate between-seed variance, and the per-seed ranges overlap. On the GPU the proposed model is not
bit-reproducible at a fixed seed (GATv2 scatter operations), whereas the GRU and temporal Transformer are; a reported
proposed-model run can therefore be re-trained but not re-created exactly. Split conformal intervals carry a marginal guarantee under exchangeability, which traffic series violate; the
measured coverage under congestion is 0.76--0.80. Explanations describe the trained model, with a background drawn
from the training partition; neighbour sensitivity perturbs the graph in ways the model never saw during training,
and only incoming edges can change a link's own prediction.

\textit{Knowledge, language model and agents.} The approved corpus is small and is not NYC DOT's signal-timing
policy; the project-defined policy is labelled as such. 171 of 1{,}633 chunks exceed the encoder's 256-token window.
The retrieval labels were drafted by the project without external review, so recall and MRR are an internal
consistency check. The language model is a 7B quantised model served locally; its advisories are only as good as the
evidence and the constraints that follow it. The advisory evaluation is functional (seven cases, four policies, 19
runs), not statistical, and on the mapped corridor the model proposed no signal change, so the simulation acceptance
path was not exercised by a real advisory. Two defects were found during the run and fixed before the reported
numbers were produced (the bootstrap day index, and a missing field that had silently prevented every LLM call in a
first agent pass); both superseded runs are kept as local evidence and described in \texttt{progress.md}.

\textit{Simulation.} The SUMO testbed is a scenario built from OSM geometry with declared demand and default signal
programs, not a calibrated digital twin, and contains no pedestrian demand; only one of the two mapped DOT links is a
forecastable one and the scenarios' demand and congestion flags are declared rather than derived from its
observations, so the testbed is a transfer demonstration. The first 45-run comparison used SUMO's insertion
routing and a stub entry edge and is superseded. Its corrected protocols use the only weekday automated count on the
corridor (2014, one direction) and then two-direction classification counts from two weekdays in April 2025; side-street
demand has no count. Signal programs are generated by netconvert or re-timed by Webster's method, not NYC timing plans,
and are uncoordinated although West Street is timed for 25\,mph progression; lane restrictions from OpenStreetMap are
dropped, which can overstate capacity. Under every protocol the testbed fails its baseline test, and with the 2025
counts it runs at 5.8\,mph where the mapped link is observed at 10.95\,mph. The preregistered split calibration (protocol v5)
raised the simulated speed to at most 8.1\,mph within its green-factor grid; the second protocol (v6) reached 10.08\,mph
with a 90\,s common cycle and 25\,mph southbound offsets, so the comparison ran on that plan. That plan is a calibrated
scenario testbed, not NYC timing: it is tuned to one observed speed on one link, progresses one direction only, and the
only admissible side-street level is zero, so side-road effects are not evaluated. The actuated controller runs on
netconvert's actuated programs rather than the calibrated plan and is an uncalibrated reference. Under the stress
scenarios 340 to 540 of 6{,}712 trips remain unfinished with either matched controller. The reported improvement of the
forecast-informed rule is a simulated result under these conditions and is not a real-world gain.
\textit{Engineering.} Data download, training, LLM inference and the simulation runs were executed on the
authors' workstation (torch 2.11.0+cu128, a recorded deviation from the lock file; from 28 October 2026 Windows
Application Control blocked the unsigned \texttt{torch\_scatter} and \texttt{torch\_sparse} extensions, which the
models do not use, so they were removed from that environment and PyTorch Geometric Temporal loads through the
documented direct-module route). On 2026-10-20 the development
container could not reach the data portals or the model server; on 2026-10-21 it could reach NYC Open Data, Crossref
and the OSM editing API (not Overpass), and the testbed audit ran there with the same SUMO version. The container
images were built and smoke-tested on the workstation only. Model weights, prediction arrays, raw data and the FAISS
index are not in the repository; their hashes and run ids are. The complete, maintained list is in
\texttt{reports/limitations.md}.
