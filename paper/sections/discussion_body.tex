\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
The system is designed so that each component's contribution can be isolated: baselines and the proposed model share
one test mask, ablations remove one context group or the gate at a time, and the advisory evaluation runs the same
cases under four policies that switch off retrieval, the constraint layer or the simulation gate. Read together, the
results support some of the proposals in Section~\ref{sec:intro} and not others.

\textit{Forecasting.} The accuracy gain of the proposed model over persistence (7--16\,\% MAE) is real and consistent
across horizons, and its margin over the tree baselines is consistent in sign (0.05--0.25\,mph) though at 15\,min no
larger than the GRU margin. Over three seeds its margin over the temporal-only Transformer
(0.04--0.08\,mph) and over the GRU at 30 and 60 minutes (0.03\,mph) is consistent across test days, while the GRU is
better at 15 minutes; these are small effects from three seeds, not a decisive architectural advantage. Two measured facts explain why the spatial layer adds little here: the graph
is sparse (16 nodes with usable data, most of them limited-access highway segments whose monitored neighbours are
kilometres away) and the explanations attribute 0.52\,mph of mean absolute effect to neighbour traffic against
8.22\,mph to the link's own last half hour. The neighbour-removal analysis shows the layer is not inert (prediction
changes of 0.7--1.3\,mph on four of the five connected links, none on the fifth) but also that removing edges sometimes lowers the error, so on this
network message passing is a modest and mixed contribution. The availability gate is the component the ablations do
support: removing it costs 0.067\,mph with an interval that excludes zero.

\textit{Context.} The weather ablation is the clearest negative result: the operational re-forecast weather features
raise MAE by 0.046\,mph, and the retrospective track, which uses reanalysis weather, is only marginally better than
the operational one. On a highway-heavy link set over nearly ten months the weather signal reaching a 5-minute speed is
weak relative to the traffic history, and a re-forecast proxy adds noise. The 311 infrastructure group helps by a
small but non-zero amount. Whether the events and incidents groups help cannot be answered on the operational track,
where the data to compute them were not available at prediction time.

\textit{Uncertainty.} The conformal intervals hold their marginal 90\,\% guarantee but are wide and under-cover
congested targets (0.76--0.80), which is exactly the regime an advisory cares about. The deterministic gate turns
this into deferrals: at the configured missing-input threshold about a quarter of forecasts (25.5--26.1\,\%) are
withheld and the MAE of the remainder is 15--22\,\% lower than that of the full set (3.72 against 4.77\,mph at
15\,min, 5.25 against 6.15 at 60\,min). For an
operator this is the honest trade-off; a calibration conditional on congestion state is the obvious next step.

\textit{Grounding and constraints.} The policy ablation isolates two mechanisms. Retrieval removes fabricated
citations (three of three without it, none of nine with it), and the citation check catches them when they occur.
The constraint layer, not the language model, decides admissibility: on an unmapped link the model produced the same
candidate (monitor-only, citing the same two manual chunks) under both policies, and it is rejected with
constraints and forwarded to human review without them. What the run does not show is a signal-timing
recommendation surviving the whole chain into simulation: on the mapped corridor the model chose monitoring only,
so the simulation gate's acceptance rule has been exercised by tests, not by a real advisory. We report this as a
gap rather than fill it with a hand-written candidate.

\textit{Simulation.} The controller comparison is the clearest example of a result that a less careful reading
would have turned into a gain: in the first 45 runs the forecast-informed rule shows 5 to 10\,\% lower time loss per
completed trip in the two stress scenarios, but it completes fewer trips under an oversaturated corridor. Auditing
the testbed instead of the controllers changed the diagnosis twice. The first suspect, the declared demand, was not
the cause: the observed weekday West Street count (1{,}389\,veh/h) is higher than the declared 1{,}200. What the
audit found was a testbed that was not measuring the corridor at all, because SUMO's insertion routing sent half of
the mainline through the Lower Manhattan grid, and, once routes were fixed, a corridor whose generated signal timing
gives the northbound mainline about 1{,}000\,veh/h where 1{,}389 are observed. Re-timing the programs with Webster's
method serves the northbound flow but leaves a southbound delay the audit cannot explain, so the testbed still fails
its own baseline. A controller comparison on such a testbed would rank signal plans by how they cope with modelling
artefacts, so the preregistered selection rule stops it, and the advisory gate refuses to judge candidates on it. A comparison with
observed speed confirmed the diagnosis: with recent counts the modelled corridor runs at about half the observed PM-peak
speed. The lesson for simulation-gated advisory is that the gate's testbed needs its own acceptance test, run on the
baseline alone and checked against observed traffic, before any candidate is scored against it. What finally passed
that test was not more green but coordination: a common cycle with 25\,mph offsets along the mapped direction took
the modelled link from 8.9 to 10.1\,mph where every split-only cell had stalled, which is also what West Street is
timed for in reality. On that plan the matched comparison does show the forecast-informed rule ahead of fixed time
under stress, by about 5\,\% of time loss with more trips completed in every seed, and we read it narrowly: a
deterministic rule that extends corridor green when congestion is forecast helps a corridor that is oversaturated by
a configured surge, in a testbed tuned to one observed speed, with no side-street traffic to lose. It says nothing
about the language model, which never proposed the action, and nothing about a real street.

\textit{What is not claimed.} Nothing here is a deployment result, a causal explanation, or a congestion reduction
on a real street. The language model never holds safety authority (constraints and the simulation gate are code),
retrieved text is untrusted evidence that must be cited by chunk id, and the most permissive status the workflow can
reach is approval for simulation, never deployment.
